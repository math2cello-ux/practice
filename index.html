Research Framing

Core Question

Do high-energy-burden communities receive fewer benefits?

The strongest version of this project asks whether high-energy- burden communities in the Boston area have lower access to clean energy infrastructure and lower adoption of clean energy technologies after accounting for income, housing tenure, building type, vehicle access, and neighborhood characteristics.

Infrastructure

Public EV charging station counts, port counts, fast charging availability, and distance to the nearest public charger.

Adoption

Residential solar installations, installed capacity, EV rebate activity, and clean heating program uptake.

Equity Context

Energy burden, rentership, multifamily housing, vehicle access, environmental justice status, and income.

What Has Been Accomplished

Project Progress

Research scope defined

The project now separates clean energy access into EV charging, solar adoption, and program benefit uptake instead of treating clean energy use as one broad variable.

Key confounders identified

The plan recognizes that income, homeownership, renter status, building type, and vehicle ownership may shape both energy burden and clean energy adoption.

Prototype map and data stack assembled

The current MapLibre prototype combines DOE LEAD energy burden, ACS and SVI tract context, Massachusetts EV charging stations, MassGIS environmental justice block groups, and municipal MassCEC solar summaries.

Analysis deliverables queued

The next deliverable is an exploratory Greater Boston tract analysis with maps, regressions, and a policy memo.

Hypotheses

What The Study Will Test

H1

Higher energy burden is associated with lower public EV charging access.

H2

Higher energy burden is associated with lower residential solar adoption.

H3

Homeownership, housing type, vehicle access, and income partly explain the relationship.

H4

Heat pump and weatherization programs may be more equitably distributed than EV and rooftop solar benefits.

Methods

Analysis Workflow

1

Build tract dataset

Join DOE LEAD, ACS controls, tract boundaries, and environmental justice classifications by GEOID.

2

Process spatial access

Spatially join charger and solar points to tracts and calculate counts, rates, capacity, and nearest-charger distance.

3

Compare outcomes

Map energy burden, EV access, and solar adoption; compare high- burden and low-burden tracts.

4

Model relationships

Estimate regression models with controls for income, rentership, housing type, vehicle ownership, and density.
